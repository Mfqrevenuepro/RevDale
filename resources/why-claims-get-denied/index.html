Why medical claims get denied (and how to prevent it)

Most denials come from a small number of causes, and most of them can be prevented before the claim is ever sent. Here is where to look.

Rejection or denial? They are not the same

  • A rejection means the claim never entered the payer's system, usually because of a formatting or data error caught by the clearinghouse or the payer's front-end edits. It can be fixed and resubmitted, and there is no appeal because it was never processed.
  • A denial means the payer processed the claim and refused to pay all or part of it. A denial may be corrected and resubmitted or appealed, depending on the reason.

Tracking both separately matters, because they have different fixes and different deadlines.

The most common reasons, and how to prevent each

  • Eligibility and registration errors. Inactive coverage, a misspelled name, a wrong date of birth or member ID, the wrong payer, or coordination of benefits that was never sorted out. Prevent it: verify coverage before every visit and scan the insurance card.
  • Missing authorization or referral. The plan required approval and none was on file, or the approval did not match the service. Prevent it: track requirements by payer and procedure, and record authorization details at scheduling.
  • Coding and documentation problems. A diagnosis that does not support the procedure, a missing modifier, services bundled together, wrong units, or notes that do not support the level billed. Prevent it: use claim checks before submission and review notes against the codes.
  • Duplicate claims. The same service submitted twice. Prevent it: track claim status before resubmitting.
  • Missed timely filing. Payers set deadlines for submitting claims. Medicare generally allows 12 months from the date of service, while commercial and Medicaid plans set their own limits, which can be much shorter. Prevent it: monitor claims that have not been accepted or paid well before the deadline.
  • Provider enrollment issues. The provider was not enrolled with the payer, or not yet effective, on the date of service, or an NPI or tax ID does not match. Prevent it: confirm effective dates in writing and keep credentialing current. See our credentialing service.
  • Coverage and medical necessity. The service is not covered, exceeds a frequency limit, or the diagnosis does not meet coverage criteria. Prevent it: check benefits and coverage policies before services are performed.

How to read the codes on your remittance

Your remittance advice lists a group code and a reason code for each adjustment. The group code says who is responsible: CO for contractual obligations, which generally cannot be billed to the patient, and PR for patient responsibility. These are some of the claim adjustment reason codes you will see often:

CodeCommon meaning
16The claim lacks information or has a submission or billing error.
18Exact duplicate claim or service.
27Expenses were incurred after coverage ended.
29The time limit for filing has expired.
50Not deemed medically necessary by the payer.
97The benefit is included in the payment for another service already adjudicated.
109The claim is not covered by this payer, so it should be sent to the correct payer.
197Precertification, authorization, or notification is absent.

Remark codes add detail. Always read the code together with the remark and the payer's own explanation.

What to do when a claim is denied

  1. Read the remittance and identify the exact reason.
  2. Decide the route. If the cause was an error on your side, correct it and resubmit. If the payer is wrong, appeal.
  3. Check the deadline. Appeal windows vary by payer and contract. For Medicare Fee-for-Service, a redetermination request is due within 120 days of receiving the initial determination. Find the deadline for each payer right away.
  4. Gather evidence. Clinical notes, authorization records, and the payer's own policy.
  5. Write a focused appeal that states the reason for denial and why the payer's own rules support payment.
  6. Track the outcome and record the reason, so patterns show up.

A prevention checklist

  • Verify eligibility and benefits before each visit.
  • Collect and scan current insurance cards and photo ID.
  • Check authorization and referral requirements when scheduling.
  • Run claims through checks for missing data, modifiers, and bundling before submission.
  • Confirm provider enrollment and effective dates for every payer.
  • Monitor claims for timely filing, not just for payment.
  • Log every denial by payer and reason, and review the pattern monthly.
  • Feed what you learn back to the front desk and the providers.

Common questions

How long do I have to appeal a denied claim?

It varies by payer and contract. For Medicare Fee-for-Service, a redetermination request is due within 120 days of receiving the initial claim determination. Other payers set their own windows, so check your contract and the remittance, and treat the shortest deadline as your target.

Should every denial be appealed?

No. Correct and resubmit errors that were yours, and appeal when you have evidence that payment is owed. Prioritize by dollar value and likelihood of success so effort goes where it pays back.

What is a good denial rate?

It depends on specialty and payer mix, so a single benchmark can mislead. It is more useful to track your own rate by payer and reason each month and watch the trend. Our first-time claims acceptance rate runs above 95%.
